A generative adversarial network pits two networks against each other. A generator starts from random noise and tries to forge samples that look real; a discriminator is a detective trying to tell the generator's fakes from genuine data. Each pushes the other to improve, and at equilibrium the forgeries are so good the detective can only guess. The second half of this chapter turns from one model to method: the practical, repeatable process for building any deep-learning system on a real problem — choosing the right metric, starting from a sensible baseline, deciding when more data is the answer, tuning hyperparameters, and debugging a model that fails silently. 01
Role · significance · the big idea · §20 intro

Introduction to GANs

Most networks we've met are discriminative: given an input, predict a label. A generative model does something harder — it learns the distribution of the data well enough to produce brand-new samples that could have come from it. Generative adversarial networks (Goodfellow et al., 2014) learn this distribution through a game. A generator G maps random noise z to a synthetic sample; a discriminator D tries to classify samples as real (from the dataset) or fake (from G). D is trained to catch fakes; G is trained to fool D. As the detective sharpens, the forger must improve, and vice versa — until, at the ideal equilibrium, G's samples match the true data distribution and D can do no better than a coin flip. No explicit likelihood is ever written down; the two networks supply each other's training signal.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine an art forger and a detective. The forger paints fakes and slips them into a gallery of real paintings; the detective's job is to spot which are fake. Every time the detective catches a fake, the forger learns what gave it away and paints better. Every time a fake sneaks through, the detective studies harder.

Round after round, the fakes get so good that the detective is basically guessing. At that point the forger has learned to paint like the real artists — which is exactly what we wanted: a machine that can make new, realistic examples on its own.

Discriminative vs generative
  • Discriminative — models P(label | input); answers "what is this?" — classifiers, detectors, taggers.
  • Generative — models the data itself; answers "what does a plausible example look like?" — and can sample new ones.
  • GAN's twist — learns to generate without an explicit density, by beating a learned critic instead of maximising a likelihood.
  • Why it's powerful — the critic is adaptive, so the training signal keeps getting sharper as the generator improves.
The two players Opposite goals

Generator G(z) — turns a random latent vector into a sample; wants D to label its output "real". Never sees real data directly, only D's feedback.

Discriminator D(x) — outputs the probability that x is real; wants to assign 1 to real data and 0 to fakes.

Equilibrium — when pgen = pdata, the best D can output is 0.5 everywhere: the fakes are indistinguishable.

02
The minimax game · §20.1

GAN Architecture & the Minimax Game

Formally, GAN training is a two-player minimax game over a single value function. The discriminator maximises its ability to tell real from fake; the generator minimises that same quantity. Training alternates: hold G fixed and take a gradient step to improve D, then hold D fixed and take a step to improve G. For a fixed generator, the optimal discriminator is D*(x) = pdata(x) / (pdata(x) + pgen(x)). Plugging that back in shows the generator is effectively minimising the Jensen–Shannon divergence between the generated and real distributions — minimised (to the value −log 4) exactly when the two distributions coincide. In practice we replace G's saturating loss with the "non-saturating" version (maximise log D(G(z))) so the generator gets strong gradients early, when its samples are still easy to catch.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: it's a tug-of-war with one rope. The detective pulls to make its "real vs fake" score as high as possible; the forger pulls to drag that same score down. They take turns tugging. If you froze the forger and let the detective play perfectly, its best rule would be "how much of the stuff here is real versus fake at this spot?"

The forger wins the game not by any trick but by making its fakes land in exactly the same places as the real data. When the two piles sit perfectly on top of each other, the detective's best guess everywhere is "50/50" — game over, forgery perfected.

In practice Non-saturating loss

Early in training G's samples are obviously fake, so log(1 − D(G(z))) saturates and G gets almost no gradient. The standard fix is to train G to maximise log D(G(z)) instead — the same fixed point, but strong gradients when G needs them most.

03
Making GANs work on images · §20.2

DCGAN

The original GAN used fully-connected nets and was notoriously unstable. The Deep Convolutional GAN (DCGAN) (Radford et al., 2015) was the recipe that made image GANs reliable, and its architectural guidelines are still a sensible default. The generator turns a latent vector into an image with a stack of fractionally-strided (transposed) convolutions that upsample — the spatial size grows layer by layer while the channel depth shrinks, ending in a full-resolution image. The discriminator is a mirror-image CNN that downsamples to a single real/fake score. The key rules: replace pooling with strided convolutions, use batch normalisation in both nets, drop fully-connected hidden layers, use ReLU in the generator (with tanh at the output) and LeakyReLU in the discriminator.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: a normal CNN shrinks a big picture down to a label. The generator does the opposite — it starts from a tiny bundle of numbers and grows it, step by step, into a full picture: 4×4, then 8×8, then 16×16, and so on, adding detail each time while using fewer "colour channels" as it goes.

DCGAN is basically a checklist that keeps this growing-a-picture process from falling apart: use the right building blocks, keep the numbers well-behaved with batch-norm, and pick the right squashing functions. Follow the checklist and the training stops exploding.

04
Taming an unstable game · §20.3

GAN Hacks & Training Stability

GANs are famously hard to train because they optimise a moving target: as G changes, D's job changes, and the game can oscillate or collapse instead of converging. The most infamous failure is mode collapse — the generator discovers one or a few outputs that reliably fool D and produces only those, ignoring the rest of the data distribution (all faces, but the same face). A large toolbox of practical "hacks" keeps training on the rails: one-sided label smoothing (label real examples 0.9 not 1.0), feature matching and minibatch discrimination (so the generator is rewarded for variety), keeping G and D roughly balanced in strength, careful learning-rate choices (often different rates for G and D), and using architectures like DCGAN. None of these are guarantees — they are empirical rules that make a difficult optimisation more reliable.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: the forger might find one "perfect fake" that always fools the detective and just make that same painting over and over. It wins the game but it's useless — it only knows how to paint one thing. That's mode collapse.

The hacks are little rules that stop this: reward the forger for variety, don't let the detective get too strong too fast, and keep both of them improving at a similar pace. Flip the fixes on below and watch the fakes spread out to cover every kind of real example instead of piling onto one.

What GANs are good for · §20.4

Applications of GANs

Because a trained generator is a smooth map from a latent space to realistic samples, GANs unlocked a wide range of applications. They synthesise photorealistic images and faces; perform image-to-image translation (sketch→photo, day→night, maps↔satellite via pix2pix and CycleGAN); do super-resolution (SRGAN) and inpainting; enable text-to-image synthesis; generate data augmentation and privacy-preserving synthetic datasets for medicine; support style transfer and creative tools; and even help with anomaly detection by modelling "normal" data. A characteristic and useful property is that the latent space is continuous and semantically meaningful: walking a straight line between two latent vectors morphs one realistic sample smoothly into another, and directions in that space can correspond to interpretable attributes.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: once the forger is good, its "bundle of numbers" input works like a set of dials. Turn the dials a little and the picture changes a little; slide smoothly from one setting to another and the picture morphs smoothly — a young face into an old one, a sketch into a photo. That smooth "space of settings" is what makes GANs so useful for editing and creating.

People use this to turn sketches into photos, make blurry images sharp, invent training data when real data is scarce, and build creative tools. Drag the slider below to walk between two settings and watch one sample melt into another.

Design process · pick the right target · §11.1

Performance Metrics

The first step of any practical deep-learning project is to decide what to measure — and it is more subtle than "accuracy". The metric must reflect the real goal, and the goal sets a target level of performance worth aiming for. When classes are imbalanced or errors have unequal costs, accuracy misleads: a detector for a rare disease can be 99% accurate by always saying "healthy". Precision (of the things you flagged, how many were right) and recall (of the things you should have flagged, how many you caught) trade off against each other as you move the decision threshold, and the F-score summarises the balance. Some tasks care about coverage (letting the model abstain when unsure), others about ranking (AUC). Choosing and justifying this metric up front is what keeps the rest of the project honest.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine testing for a rare illness that only 1 in 100 people have. A lazy test that always says "you're fine" is right 99% of the time — but it never catches a single sick person. "Percent correct" hid a useless test.

So we ask two better questions instead. Precision: when the test rings the alarm, how often is it truly right? Recall: of all the people who are actually sick, how many did it catch? Move the alarm's sensitivity below and watch these two trade off — make it jumpier and you catch more sick people but cry wolf more often.

Design process · start simple · §11.2

Default Baseline Models

Once you have a metric, resist the urge to invent something clever. Establish a sensible baseline first, driven by the data type. For fixed-size vectors, a feed-forward net with ReLU units is the default; for images or grid data, a convolutional network; for sequences, a gated recurrent net (LSTM/GRU) or, increasingly, an attention model. Reach for reliable defaults everywhere else too: SGD with momentum or Adam as the optimiser, batch normalisation to stabilise training, and early stopping as free, effective regularisation — adding dropout if you see overfitting. Also ask whether deep learning is even needed: if a simple model or a known algorithm solves it, use that. The baseline exists to be measured against, so you can tell whether later complexity actually helps.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: before building a fancy race car, build a plain, reliable car that runs. It tells you the road, the rules, and how fast "normal" is. Then, every fancy part you add, you can check: did it actually make things better than the plain car?

There are well-tested "plain cars" for each kind of data: a straightforward net for tables, a picture-net for images, a memory-net for sequences — plus safe default settings for the engine. Start there, measure, and only add complexity that earns its keep.

Design process · the data question · §11.3

Determining Whether to Gather More Data

After a baseline runs, a common instinct is "get more data" — but that is not always the right move, and data is expensive. The disciplined procedure reads the two error numbers. If training error is high, the model isn't even fitting what it has: more data won't help — increase capacity, tune hyperparameters, or fix the optimisation first. If training error is low but test error is high (a large generalisation gap), the model overfits: now gathering more data is a strong option, alongside regularisation. When you do gather data, think in terms of the learning curve — plot performance against dataset size to estimate how much more you'd need to hit the target, since gains usually diminish. Doubling the data for a 1% improvement may not be worth it.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: if a student can't even do the practice problems they've already seen, giving them more practice problems won't help — they need better teaching or more effort first. But if they ace the practice yet flunk the real test, that's a sign they memorised too narrowly, and seeing more varied examples really would help.

So read the two scores. Bad on practice (training) → don't gather data, fix the model. Good on practice but bad on the test → more data is worth considering. Slide the knobs below and the recommended move updates.

Design process · tuning · §11.4

Selecting Hyperparameters

Deep models have many knobs — learning rate, capacity, regularisation strength, batch size — and setting them is much of the practical work. The single most important is the learning rate: too low and training crawls or gets stuck; too high and the loss oscillates or diverges; the useful range is a valley in between, giving a characteristic U-shaped curve of validation error against learning rate. Two broad strategies exist. Manual tuning uses understanding: most hyperparameters control effective capacity, and the goal is to sit near the bottom of the U — reduce error by adding capacity or lowering regularisation when underfitting, and the reverse when overfitting. Automatic search — grid or random — removes the guesswork; notably, random search usually beats grid search because it samples the few important dimensions far more densely.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: the learning rate is how big a step the model takes while learning. Tiny steps mean you crawl and might never arrive; giant steps mean you leap right over the goal and bounce around. There's a "just right" middle — a valley — and most tuning is about finding it.

You can hunt by hand (nudge a knob, check the score, repeat) or let the computer try many settings. A neat trick: trying random settings usually beats a neat evenly-spaced grid, because it explores the knob that actually matters far more thoroughly.

Design process · when it fails silently · §11.5

Debugging Strategies

Deep models fail in a uniquely frustrating way: they rarely crash, they just quietly perform badly, and you usually don't know the right answer to compare against. A handful of strategies cut through the fog. Visualise the model in action — look at its actual outputs, not just the loss. Visualise the worst mistakes to find systematic errors (e.g. mislabelled data). Use a software test: can the model overfit a tiny dataset? If it can't drive training loss to near zero on ten examples, there's a bug, not a data problem. Monitor histograms of activations and gradients to catch dead units or saturation. And the classic: a gradient check — compare your analytic back-prop gradients against a numerical finite-difference estimate; if they disagree, the gradient code is wrong. Choosing a good step size ε for that check matters: too large and the approximation is crude, too small and floating-point round-off dominates.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: a broken deep model usually doesn't beep or stop — it just gives so-so answers, and you often can't tell if it's a little wrong or badly wrong. So you use detective tricks: actually look at what it produces, stare at its worst mistakes, and check whether it can memorise a tiny handful of examples (if it can't, something's genuinely broken).

One favourite: check the math. You can estimate a slope two ways — the formula the code uses, and a "wiggle it a tiny bit and see how much it changes" estimate. If the two don't match, there's a bug. But the wiggle size matters: too big is inaccurate, too tiny and the computer's rounding wrecks it. Find the sweet spot below.

A debugging checklist
  • Overfit a tiny set — if training loss won't approach zero on a handful of examples, it's a bug, not the data.
  • Visualise outputs & worst errors — read the model's actual predictions; systematic mistakes often reveal bad labels or preprocessing.
  • Watch activation/gradient histograms — spot dead ReLUs, saturated sigmoids, or exploding/vanishing gradients.
  • Gradient check — compare analytic and numerical derivatives on a small model with a well-chosen ε.
  • Compare train vs test error — separates optimisation problems (high train error) from generalisation problems (high gap).

Putting it together · §11 overview

The End-to-End Design Process

The pieces combine into a single, repeatable recipe for attacking a real problem. (1) Determine goals — choose the performance metric and a target level tied to the actual application. (2) Build an end-to-end pipeline fast — get a sensible baseline running and measured as soon as possible, however rough. (3) Instrument & diagnose — read train vs test error, visualise outputs and worst mistakes, and identify the real bottleneck rather than guessing. (4) Iterate with targeted changes — based on the diagnosis, make one change at a time: gather more data if the gap is large, tune hyperparameters (learning rate first) if it underfits, or change the algorithm if the approach is wrong — then remeasure. Most of the value comes not from a clever architecture but from disciplined, evidence-driven iteration.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: it's a loop, not a straight line. First decide how you'll keep score. Then build the simplest thing that works end-to-end, and measure it. Then look closely to find the one thing holding it back. Then fix that one thing and measure again. Repeat.

The secret isn't a magic model — it's this honest cycle: measure, find the real problem, make one change, measure again. Click through the steps and try a few "what's wrong?" scenarios below to see which action each one calls for.

If you remember only one thing

This chapter has two lessons. First, a GAN learns to generate by competition: a generator forges samples from noise while a discriminator learns to catch them, and at equilibrium the fakes match the real distribution and the critic is reduced to guessing — with DCGAN's recipe and a bag of stabilising hacks making it work in practice. Second, building deep models is a disciplined process, not a lucky guess: choose a metric that reflects the goal, start from a sensible baseline, read train-versus-test error to decide whether to gather data or tune, treat the learning rate as the key knob, debug by making the model overfit a tiny set and gradient-checking, and iterate one change at a time. Method beats cleverness.
