Training a neural network is an optimization problem — but an unusual one. We don't want the true minimum of the training loss; we want the parameters that generalize, reached by a noisy, high-dimensional descent riddled with saddle points, cliffs, flat plateaus and gradients that vanish through depth. This volume is the machinery that makes it work: stochastic gradient descent, momentum, careful initialization, adaptive per-parameter rates like Adam, layer-wise pretraining and adversarial hardening. 01
The mismatch · §8.1

How Learning Differs from Pure Optimization

Optimization minimizes a function; learning wants a function it can't even measure. We would love to minimize the true risk — the expected loss over the real data distribution — but we only have samples, so we minimize the empirical risk over the training set and hope the two agree. That single gap, plus a few others, is what separates training a network from solving a clean optimization problem.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine studying for a test using an old practice sheet. Your real goal is to do well on the real test next week, but the only thing you can actually practise on is the sheet in front of you. If you memorise the practice sheet perfectly, you might still flop on the real test.

Machines have the exact same problem. The "real test" is every future example the world will ever throw at it — which nobody can show the computer. So it practises on the examples we do have (the training set) and tries to keep the gap between practice and reality small. It also uses a couple of clever cheats: it grades itself with a smoother, easier-to-improve score than the real "right or wrong" count, and it peeks at practice questions in small handfuls rather than all at once.

True risk vs empirical risk
J*(θ) = E(x,y)∼pdata [ L(f(x;θ), y) ]   → can't compute   ⟹    J(θ) = (1/m) Σi L(f(x(i);θ), y(i))   over m training examples
Four ways learning ≠ optimization
  • Objective is a proxy = we minimize empirical risk on a finite sample, not the true risk we care about. Minimizing it too hard is overfitting.
  • Surrogate loss = the loss we actually care about (e.g. 0–1 classification error) has zero gradient almost everywhere, so we optimize a smooth surrogate like log-loss instead.
  • Stop early = optimization halts when the gradient is small; learning halts when a validation metric stops improving — often long before the training loss bottoms out.
  • Minibatches = we estimate the gradient from a small random batch, trading exactness for many cheap, noisy steps.
Why a surrogate loss Gradients need to flow

The 0–1 loss counts mistakes: it is flat between mistakes and jumps at the boundary, so its gradient is 0 almost everywhere and undefined at the jump. Gradient descent has nothing to follow.

A surrogate — negative log-likelihood, hinge, squared error — is smooth and keeps pushing even once the sign is already correct, driving the model to be confidently right. It upper-bounds the real loss, so making it small makes the real error small too.

Surrogate loss bench Loss vs classification margin y·f(x)
Loss (vertical) against margin m = y·f(x). Right of 0 = correct; left = wrong.
  • 0–1 loss (grey) = a step: 1 when wrong, 0 when right. Flat gradient everywhere — useless for descent.
  • Log / logistic (violet) = smooth, never quite zero; keeps rewarding larger correct margins, so it makes the model confidently right.
  • Hinge / SVM (teal) = zero once the margin exceeds 1, a constant slope before that — the max-margin idea.
  • Squared (rose) = penalizes even over-confident correct predictions, so it's rarely used raw for classification.
The takeaway. Each surrogate sits above the 0–1 step, so shrinking it shrinks the true error — but with a gradient the optimizer can actually follow.
In practice Every training loop

This mismatch is why your training curve and your validation curve diverge. Training loss keeps sliding down as the optimizer overfits the finite sample; validation loss bottoms out and turns up. You watch the validation curve, not the training loss, to decide when the model is actually good.

It's also why classification models are trained with cross-entropy, not accuracy: accuracy has no usable gradient. You optimize the surrogate and report the metric you care about.

Rule of thumb: train on a smooth surrogate, early-stop on the real validation metric, and never confuse "training loss is tiny" with "the model is good".
02
The terrain · §8.2

Challenges in Neural Network Optimization

Neural-network loss surfaces are non-convex and high-dimensional, and they fight back. The classic worry was local minima, but the real obstacles turn out to be saddle points, flat plateaus, ill-conditioning, cliffs and vanishing gradients. Knowing which enemy you face tells you which tool from the rest of this chapter to reach for.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: think of the loss as a giant hilly landscape in the fog, and the optimizer as a hiker who can only feel the slope right under their feet and always steps downhill. Lots of things can go wrong: a flat meadow where every direction feels level (which way is down?), a mountain pass that dips one way but rises another, or a sudden cliff that sends the hiker tumbling.

Once upon a time people feared the hiker would get stuck in a small dip (a "local minimum") and miss the deepest valley. It turns out that in a landscape with millions of directions, truly-stuck dips are rare — the real time-wasters are the flat meadows and mountain passes where the ground barely tilts, so the hiker crawls. Most of this chapter is about helping the hiker cross those flat spots quickly and safely.

Ill-conditioning

The Hessian has wildly different curvatures across directions. Gradient descent then bounces across steep directions while crawling along flat ones — even a convex bowl becomes painfully slow. Momentum and adaptive rates target exactly this.

Saddles > local minima

In high dimensions, critical points are overwhelmingly saddle points, not minima. The gradient nearly vanishes, stalling first-order methods, but the loss there is usually far above the global minimum — so escaping matters.

Cliffs & explosions

Sharp cliffs — common in RNNs — produce huge gradients that fling the parameters far away in one step. Gradient clipping caps the step size so a single spike can't wreck the run.

In practice Diagnosing a stalled run

When training plateaus, the map above is your diagnostic. Loss flat but gradient tiny? You're likely on a saddle or plateau — momentum and adaptive methods help punch through. Loss spiking erratically? A cliff — clip the gradient or lower the rate.

Slow, zig-zagging progress on a smooth curve is the signature of ill-conditioning — the case momentum was invented for, coming up next.

Good to know: the empirical surprise of deep learning is that most local minima are nearly as good as the global one — so the fight is against flatness and conditioning, not bad minima.
03
Depth bites back · Vanishing & exploding gradients

The Difficulty of Training Deep Networks

Depth multiplies. Back-propagation sends the error signal backward through every layer, and at each layer it is multiplied by that layer's weights and activation slopes. Multiply many numbers below 1 and the signal vanishes; multiply many numbers above 1 and it explodes. Either way, the early layers of a deep network get a gradient that is useless — too small to learn from or too big to be stable.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine whispering a message down a long line of friends, where each friend can only pass on half of what they heard. After ten friends almost nothing is left — the message has faded to silence. Now imagine instead each friend doubles the volume: by the end it's a deafening, garbled roar.

The learning signal in a deep network travels backward through the layers exactly like that whispered message. If each layer shrinks it a little, the first layers hear nothing and never learn (the signal vanished). If each layer amplifies it, the signal blows up into nonsense (it exploded). The fix is to keep every layer passing the message along at roughly the same volume — which is what good activation functions and careful initialization are for.

Why the gradient scales geometrically with depth

The sweet spot. Sigmoid's slope maxes at 0.25, so its signal fades fast — a big reason ReLU (slope 1 on the active side) and near-1 weight scaling took over. Aim for a per-layer factor near 1: neither fading nor blowing up.
The historical fixes
  • Better activations = ReLU and friends keep the derivative at 1 on the active side, so the product doesn't collapse like sigmoid/tanh.
  • Careful init = Xavier/He scaling sets the initial weights so variance is preserved layer to layer (station 06).
  • Normalization = batch/layer norm rescales activations mid-network to keep signals well-behaved.

In practice Why ResNets exist

Before residual connections, networks past ~20 layers actually got worse, not from overfitting but because the gradient couldn't reach the early layers. Skip connections let signals bypass the long multiplicative chain, and suddenly 50-, 100-, even 1000-layer networks trained fine.

In recurrent networks the same problem appears over time steps — the reason plain RNNs forget long contexts and LSTMs/GRUs (with their gated, near-1 pathways) were invented.

Where it shows up: ResNets in vision, LSTM/GRU gating and gradient clipping in sequence models, and normalization layers nearly everywhere.
04
The workhorse · §8.3

Stochastic Gradient Descent

Stochastic gradient descent is the engine of nearly all deep learning. Instead of the exact gradient over the whole dataset, it uses a cheap, noisy estimate from a small random minibatch, takes a step, and repeats. The noise is not just tolerated — it's useful, jostling the iterate out of shallow traps — but it means the learning rate must eventually shrink for the run to settle.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: you're finding the lowest point in a valley in thick fog. The careful way is to survey the whole valley before every step — accurate, but painfully slow. The stochastic way is to glance at just a small patch near your feet and step right away. Each glance is a bit wrong, so you wander a little, but you take hundreds of steps in the time the careful method takes one.

That's SGD. The "small patch" is a minibatch of examples. The steps are jittery because each batch gives a slightly different idea of "downhill," but the jitter actually helps you hop over tiny ditches. Near the bottom, though, the jitter keeps you bouncing around the lowest point, so you slowly take smaller and smaller steps to settle down — that shrinking step size is the learning-rate schedule.

SGD update on a minibatch of size b
ĝ = (1/b) Σi∈batch ∇θ L(f(x(i);θ), y(i))   ⟹   θ ← θ − εk ĝ  , with εk decayed over time
The two knobs that decide everything
  • Learning rate ε = the single most important hyperparameter. Too small: glacial. Too big: overshoot and diverge. Usually decayed on a schedule (linear, cosine, step).
  • Batch size b = bigger batches give a less noisy gradient (noise ∝ 1/√b) but cost more per step and can generalize slightly worse. Small batches are noisier but explore more.
  • Decay the rate = a sufficient condition for convergence is Σεk = ∞ and Σεk² < ∞ — step forever, but with shrinking steps.
  • Cost per step is constant = SGD's compute per update doesn't grow with dataset size, which is why it scales to millions of examples.
Why the noise helps and why it must shrink

Because the gradient is estimated from a random batch, even at the true minimum ĝ is non-zero — so a fixed rate leaves the iterate rattling in a cloud around the optimum whose size is set by ε.

Shrinking ε tightens that cloud toward a point. Early on, the same noise is a feature: it lets SGD escape sharp, shallow minima and drift toward the broad, flat basins that tend to generalize better.

In practice Default optimizer

SGD with momentum (next section) remains a top choice for training large vision models — often generalizing better than fancier methods when tuned. The recipe is a learning-rate warmup, then a cosine or step decay, with batch sizes chosen to fill the hardware.

The linear scaling rule — double the batch, double the learning rate — lets teams train across hundreds of GPUs while keeping the dynamics roughly fixed.

Where it shows up: essentially every training run on Earth; the differences between optimizers are refinements on top of this loop.

Build up speed · §8.3.2–8.3.3

Momentum & Nesterov Momentum

Plain SGD zig-zags across a stretched valley, wasting steps bouncing between the steep walls while barely moving along the gentle floor. Momentum fixes this by giving the iterate velocity: it accumulates a running average of past gradients, so consistent directions build up speed while oscillating ones cancel out. Nesterov's variant looks ahead before it commits, correcting sooner.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: roll a heavy ball down a long, narrow half-pipe. A weightless dot would ping-pong from wall to wall and creep forward slowly. A heavy ball, though, keeps rolling in whatever direction it's already going — the side-to-side wobbles cancel out because it can't change direction instantly, while its speed down the pipe just keeps building.

Momentum gives the optimizer that heaviness. It remembers where it was heading and keeps some of that motion, so back-and-forth wobbles average away and steady downhill progress speeds up. Nesterov momentum is a slightly smarter ball: before it fully commits to a step, it peeks a little ahead to where its speed is carrying it and checks the slope there, so it brakes earlier and overshoots less.

Momentum (velocity v, coefficient β)
v ← βv − ε∇J(θ) ;  θ ← θ + v     Nesterov: evaluate the gradient at the look-ahead point
v ← βv − ε∇J(θ + βv) ;  θ ← θ + v
What β buys you
  • β = 0 = ordinary SGD, no memory.
  • β ≈ 0.9 = the effective step is up to 1/(1−β) ≈ 10× the raw gradient step in consistent directions — a big speed-up along the valley floor.
  • Cancels oscillation = gradients that flip sign each step average toward zero, damping the zig-zag across the walls.
  • Too high = β near 1 can overshoot and orbit the minimum; it trades responsiveness for smoothing.
Nesterov's twist

Standard momentum evaluates the gradient where it currently stands, then adds velocity. Nesterov first jumps ahead by the velocity, then measures the gradient — a partial look into the future.

That foresight lets it correct an about-to-overshoot step one iteration sooner, giving smoother, often faster convergence on well-behaved (convex) problems for the same β.

In practice The default pairing

"SGD" in a deep-learning paper almost always means SGD with momentum, β ≈ 0.9. It's the standard training recipe for convolutional networks and still competitive with adaptive methods on generalization.

Nesterov momentum is a drop-in upgrade offered by every framework; it tends to help most on smoother problems and is essentially free.

Where it shows up: torch.optim.SGD(..., momentum=0.9, nesterov=True) — one line, large payoff.

Where you start matters · §8.4

Parameter Initialization Strategies

A deep network is exquisitely sensitive to its starting weights. Start them all equal and every unit in a layer stays identical forever — no learning. Start them too large and activations saturate or explode; too small and the signal fades to nothing. Good initialization sets the scale so that the variance of activations (and of gradients) is preserved from layer to layer.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine a relay race where each runner passes a baton. If everyone runs at the exact same speed in the exact same way, they're indistinguishable — the team can never figure out who should specialise in what. And if each hand-off either drops the baton (too weak) or hurls it out of the stadium (too strong), the race falls apart.

Setting a network's starting weights is choosing how that first hand-off goes. We give each weight a small random value so no two units start identical — that's "breaking symmetry," which lets them learn different features. And we tune the size of those random values just right, so the signal is passed hand-to-hand at a steady strength all the way through, neither fading out nor blowing up.

The two jobs of initialization
  • Break symmetry = weights must be random, not constant. If two units in a layer start identical and see the same input, they receive identical gradients and stay clones forever. Never initialize all weights to zero.
  • Preserve variance = scale the random weights so a signal's variance stays roughly constant through the layers — the antidote to the vanishing/exploding problem of station 03.
  • Biases = usually fine at 0 (or a small positive value for ReLU to keep units active early).
Xavier / Glorot vs He
Xavier (tanh/sigmoid): Var(W) = 2 / (nin + nout)
He (ReLU): Var(W) = 2 / nin

Both scale the weight variance inversely with fan-in so that summing many inputs doesn't inflate the variance. He uses a factor of 2 to compensate for ReLU zeroing out half its inputs.

Modern architectures lean on normalization layers (batch/layer norm) to make training far more forgiving of the initial scale, but a sane init still helps, especially early on.

Where it shows up: nn.init.kaiming_normal_ (He) and nn.init.xavier_uniform_ — matched to your activation function.

A rate per parameter · §8.5

Adaptive Learning Rates — AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam

A single global learning rate is a compromise: too big for steep parameters, too small for flat ones. Adaptive methods give each parameter its own effective step size, automatically shrinking the step for directions with large, frequent gradients and enlarging it for rarely-updated ones. AdaGrad started the family; RMSProp fixed its fatal flaw; Adam added momentum and became the default.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine steering a shopping trolley with one wobbly wheel that pulls hard to the left and one stiff wheel that barely turns. Using the same amount of force on both is silly — you want to nudge the stiff wheel a lot and the wobbly one only gently. Adaptive methods keep a little notebook on each wheel: "this one has been jerking around a lot, so ease off; that one hardly moves, so push harder."

The "notebook" is a running tally of how big each parameter's gradients have been. AdaGrad adds every past gradient into the tally, so steps only ever get smaller — great at first, but eventually everything grinds to a halt. RMSProp lets old entries fade so the tally reflects the recent past and never freezes. Adam keeps two notebooks — one for recent speed (momentum) and one for recent bumpiness (RMSProp) — which is why it's the popular default.

The shared idea — scale each step by its own gradient history
AdaGrad: r ← r + g²  ·  RMSProp: r ← ρr + (1−ρ)g²  ·  step = −ε · g / (√r + δ)
Adam: s ← β₁s + (1−β₁)g (momentum), r ← β₂r + (1−β₂)g² ⟹ θ ← θ − ε · ŝ / (√r̂ + δ)
AdaGrad

Accumulates the sum of squared gradients. Excellent for sparse features (rare directions keep a big step), but the denominator only grows, so the effective rate decays monotonically and can stop learning too early.

RMSProp

Replaces the sum with an exponentially decaying average (ρ ≈ 0.9). Old gradients fade, so the step size adapts to the recent landscape and never permanently freezes — the workhorse fix to AdaGrad.

Adam

RMSProp + momentum, with bias-correction on both moving averages. Robust default hyperparameters (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999) make it the go-to for most models out of the box.

Which to reach for
  • Adam = the safe default — fast, forgiving, great for Transformers, GANs, RL, and anything you want to train without babysitting the schedule.
  • SGD + momentum = often the best final accuracy on vision when you can afford to tune the schedule; adaptive methods can generalize slightly worse.
  • RMSProp = a solid choice for recurrent nets and non-stationary objectives.
  • AdamW = Adam with decoupled weight decay — now standard for large-model training.
No free lunch

Adaptive methods converge fast but the minimum they find can generalize a touch worse than well-tuned SGD+momentum. There is no single best optimizer across all problems.

Practical advice: start with Adam to get moving quickly; if you're chasing the last fraction of accuracy on a vision benchmark, try tuned SGD+momentum with a good schedule.

In practice The modern default

Adam (and AdamW) trains the overwhelming majority of large models today — language models, diffusion models, most research prototypes — because it "just works" with little tuning.

Its adaptivity is especially valuable when gradients vary wildly in scale across parameters, as they do in the embedding-plus-attention stacks of large Transformers.

Where it shows up: torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4) — the "3e-4 and Adam" folk default of deep learning.

One layer at a time · §8.7.4

Greedy Layer-Wise Pretraining

Before good initialization and activations tamed deep training, there was a clever workaround: don't train the deep network all at once. Train it one layer at a time, each layer learning to represent the output of the layer below (often unsupervised), then stack them and fine-tune the whole thing end-to-end. It's called greedy because each layer is optimized on its own, and layer-wise because layers are added one by one.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: you can't build a tall tower of blocks in one heroic move — it topples. So you build it one floor at a time: lay the ground floor and make it solid, then build the next floor on top, then the next. Only once the whole tower stands do you go back and gently adjust every floor so they line up perfectly.

Greedy layer-wise pretraining builds a deep network the same patient way. First it teaches the bottom layer to find simple patterns (like edges in a picture). Then it freezes that and teaches the next layer to combine those into bigger patterns, and so on up the stack. Once all the floors are in place, a final fine-tuning pass nudges everything together toward the actual task. It was the trick that first made very deep networks trainable.

The two-phase recipe
x→Train layer 1→Freeze · train layer 2→Freeze · train layer 3→Stack all→Fine-tune end-to-end

Phase 1 — pretraining. Each layer is trained greedily, usually as an autoencoder or RBM, to reconstruct or model its input. The learned weights become the initialization for that layer.

Phase 2 — fine-tuning. The pretrained layers are assembled into one deep network and trained together with ordinary supervised backprop, now starting from a much better point than random.

Why it helped Two complementary effects
  • Better optimization = each layer starts in a sensible region, so the deep net avoids the worst plateaus and vanishing-gradient stalls of a cold random start.
  • Regularization = unsupervised pretraining biases the weights toward structure that explains the input distribution, which often transfers to the supervised task and reduces overfitting — most valuable when labels are scarce.

In practice From workaround to transfer learning

Pure greedy unsupervised pretraining faded once ReLUs, He/Xavier init, batch norm and residual connections let deep nets train from scratch. But its core idea never left: initialize from a model that already learned useful structure.

That idea is now transfer learning and self-supervised pretraining — pretrain a big model on masses of unlabeled data, then fine-tune on your task. Every fine-tuned language or vision model is greedy pretraining's intellectual descendant, scaled up.

Where it shows up: fine-tuning pretrained Transformers and vision backbones; the "pretrain then adapt" paradigm that dominates modern practice.

Train against the worst case · §7.13

Adversarial Training

Even a highly accurate network can be fooled by an input perturbation so small a human can't see it. These adversarial examples exploit the network's near-linear behaviour in high dimensions: many tiny, aligned nudges add up to a big change in the output. Adversarial training fights back by generating these attacks during training and forcing the model to classify them correctly — hardening it and, as a bonus, regularizing it.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine a test where a sneaky classmate is allowed to change your answer sheet by the tiniest amount right before grading — flipping a decimal, smudging a digit — just enough to turn a right answer wrong. If you only ever practise on clean sheets, you'll be helpless against the sneak. But if you practise with the sneak messing things up, you learn to give answers that are robust to a little tampering.

Adversarial training is practising with that sneak. During learning, the computer deliberately builds the most confusing tiny change to each example — a nudge you'd never notice with your eyes — and then insists the model still gets it right. Doing this over and over makes the model steadier: it stops relying on flimsy, easily-nudged clues and learns sturdier ones.

Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) — the classic one-step attack
xadv = x + ε · sign( ∇x L(f(x), y) )   — nudge every input pixel by ε in the direction that most increases the loss

Raise ε until the hollow point crosses the boundary — the prediction flips even though the move is tiny. Then toggle adversarial training and watch the boundary pull back to a safer margin.

Why tiny nudges work
  • Linearity in high dimensions = a network is locally near-linear, so a perturbation of ε per input, summed over thousands of inputs, shifts the pre-activation by a large amount.
  • Aligned with the gradient = the sign() attack points every pixel the exact way that raises the loss, so the nudges reinforce rather than cancel.
  • Transferability = adversarial examples often fool other models too, enabling black-box attacks — a real security concern.
What training on them buys
  • Robustness = the model classifies correctly within an ε-ball around each example, resisting the attack it was trained against.
  • Regularization = demanding a locally constant output smooths the decision surface, which can improve ordinary generalization too.
  • The cost = generating attacks each step is expensive, and robustness to one attack doesn't guarantee robustness to all — it remains an active arms race.
In practice Safety & security

Adversarial robustness matters wherever a mistake is exploitable: self-driving perception (a doctored stop sign), malware and spam filters, biometric and content-moderation systems. Attackers actively probe these models.

Multi-step attacks like PGD (iterated FGSM) are the standard strong benchmark, and PGD-based adversarial training is the leading practical defence — though robust accuracy still lags clean accuracy, keeping this a live research frontier.

Where it shows up: robustness benchmarks, security-critical vision systems, and as a general-purpose regularizer that enforces local smoothness.

If you remember only one thing

Training a deep net is noisy descent through a hostile high-dimensional landscape. The gradient is estimated (SGD), consistent directions are worth accelerating (momentum), each parameter deserves its own step (Adam), and where you start (initialization) decides whether the signal even survives the depth. Everything else — pretraining, adversarial hardening — is buying a better starting point or a smoother surface.

01

Optimize a proxy

We minimize empirical risk with a smooth surrogate loss, then early-stop on a validation metric — not the training loss.

02

Saddles, not minima

The real obstacles are saddle points, plateaus, ill-conditioning and cliffs — most local minima are nearly as good as the global one.

03

Depth multiplies signals

Gradients scale like (w·σ′)^L. Below 1 they vanish, above 1 they explode; ReLU, good init, norm and residuals keep the factor near 1.

04

SGD: cheap, noisy steps

Minibatch gradients give many fast steps; the noise aids exploration but forces a decaying learning rate to settle.

05

Momentum builds velocity

v ← βv − ε∇J accelerates consistent directions and cancels oscillation; Nesterov looks ahead to brake earlier.

06

Init breaks symmetry & keeps variance

Random weights (never all-zero) scaled by fan-in — Xavier for tanh, He for ReLU — preserve signal through the layers.

07

Adam: a rate per parameter

RMSProp's decaying gradient-square average plus momentum, bias-corrected. The forgiving default; tuned SGD can still win on vision.

08

Pretrain, then adapt

Greedy layer-wise pretraining gave deep nets a good start; its idea lives on as transfer and self-supervised learning.
