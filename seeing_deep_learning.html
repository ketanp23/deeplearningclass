Lab 01

The Neuron and its Activation

Everything in deep learning is built from one unit that does two things: it takes a weighted sum, then bends it. Understand those two steps completely and the rest of the field is composition.

↳ notebook: DL_Lab1_Neurons_and_Activations.ipynb

1.1 · Anatomy of a neuron

A neuron receives several numbers, multiplies each by its own weight, adds them together along with a bias, and passes the total through an activation function. That is the entire object. Two lines of arithmetic:

the two steps z = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + ⋯ + wₙxₙ + b
a = f(z)

The first line is linear: a weighted vote. Each weight is how much that input matters, and its sign says whether the input argues for firing or against it. The bias is the neuron's baseline eagerness — a large positive bias means it fires unless talked out of it.

The second line is the non-linear step, and it is where all the power hides. Watch the signal travel:

FIG 1.1One neuron, computing
Signal flow. Inputs arrive on the left, each edge scales its value by that weight (thickness = magnitude, violet = positive, rose = negative). The circle sums them with the bias, then squashes. Notice: flipping a weight negative reverses that input's influence entirely — the same input now argues against firing.

1.2 · Weights tilt the line, the bias slides it

With two inputs we can draw the neuron's whole behaviour on a plane. The set of points where z = 0 is a straight line — the decision boundary. On one side the neuron outputs high, on the other, low.

This gives the two parameters a crisp geometric meaning. The weight vector (w₁, w₂) is perpendicular to the line, so changing the weights rotates it. The bias controls the line's distance from the origin, so changing the bias slides it without turning it.

FIG 1.2A neuron is a line (and a soft cliff)
Left: the neuron's output across the whole input plane — bright where it fires. Right: a slice through it, showing how sharply it transitions. Notice: the weights only ever rotate the boundary; the bias only ever shifts it. A single neuron can never produce a curved boundary — no matter what you set.
Key idea

One neuron = one straight cut through the input space. Everything a network can do beyond a straight cut comes from stacking neurons and putting a non-linearity between them.

1.3 · The activation gallery

The activation f decides how the weighted sum turns into an output. But training does not care about f nearly as much as it cares about f′, its derivative — because back-propagation multiplies derivatives together. So we always plot both.

Scrub the input value below and watch the tangent line and the gradient meter. The shaded regions are the saturation zones, where the curve flattens and the gradient collapses toward zero.

FIG 1.3Activation functions and their gradients
Solid = f(x), dashed = f′(x), and the moving tangent shows the slope at your chosen input. Notice: sigmoid's gradient never exceeds 0.25, and falls below 0.01 once |x| > 4.6. ReLU's gradient is exactly 1 for every positive input — it does not decay at all. This one fact is most of the reason deep networks train today.
The trap

A ReLU whose input is always negative outputs 0 forever, and its gradient is 0 forever, so no update can ever revive it — a dead unit. Leaky ReLU keeps a small slope (0.01–0.1) on the left precisely to prevent this.

1.4 · Why we need the bend at all

Here is the argument that justifies the entire activation function. Suppose we drop it and stack two purely linear layers:

linear composed with linear a₁ = W₁x + b₁
a₂ = W₂a₁ + b₂ = W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁)x + (W₂b₁ + b₂)

The result has exactly the form Wx + b. A hundred stacked linear layers collapse into a single linear layer. Depth without non-linearity buys you nothing. Insert even one bend, and the composition can curve:

FIG 1.4Composition: with and without the bend
Left: two linear layers composed — still a straight line, always. Right: the same two layers with an activation between them — a curve that can bend, plateau and turn. Notice: as you add layers on the left, nothing new appears. On the right, each layer adds expressive detail.

1.5 · Softmax: turning scores into belief

Hidden layers bend; the output layer must speak the language of the task. For choosing among k classes we need k numbers that are positive and sum to one. Softmax does exactly that in two moves: exponentiate (making everything positive and amplifying differences), then normalize.

softmax softmax(z)ᵢ = e^{zᵢ} ⁄ Σⱼ e^{zⱼ}

The exponential is what makes softmax soft: a score that is only slightly higher wins only slightly more probability. The temperature T — dividing the scores before exponentiating — controls how decisive it is.

FIG 1.5Raw scores → exponentials → probabilities
Three stages of the same four numbers: the raw logits, their exponentials, and the normalized probabilities. Notice: raising one logit steals probability from all the others — they compete for a fixed budget of 1.0. As temperature → 0 softmax becomes a hard argmax; as T grows it flattens to a uniform guess.
Lab 02

Learning XOR — Why Depth Exists

A four-point puzzle that a single neuron provably cannot solve. Its solution is the reason hidden layers exist, and the geometric intuition it gives you — that layers bend space — carries all the way to modern architectures.

↳ notebook: DL_Lab2_Learning_XOR.ipynb

2.1 · Linearly separable, and not

Take two binary inputs and three logical functions. AND is true only at (1,1). OR is true everywhere except (0,0). XOR is true when the inputs differ. For AND and OR you can draw a straight line with all the trues on one side. For XOR — try.

FIG 2.1AND, OR, XOR
Filled dots = 1, hollow = 0. The dashed line is the best separating line a single neuron can find. Notice: XOR's two positive points sit on opposite corners. Any line that puts both of them on one side must also capture at least one negative point.

2.2 · The impossible line

Rather than take that on faith, sweep every possible line. Below, the angle and offset of a separating line are driven through their whole range, and we count how many of the four XOR points it classifies correctly. The best any line ever achieves is three out of four.

FIG 2.2Every line fails
Left: the current line and which points it gets right. Right: the running record of the best score found so far, over every angle tried. Notice: the record never reaches 4/4. This is not a training failure — no setting of w₁, w₂, b exists. In 1969 this observation stalled neural network research for a decade.

2.3 · A hidden layer bends the space

Here is the key insight, and it is worth sitting with. The hidden layer does not draw a curved boundary. It redraws the space — stretching, folding and squashing the input plane — until the four points land in a new arrangement where a single straight line does separate them. The output neuron then draws that ordinary straight line.

Press play to watch the input square deform into the hidden layer's coordinates.

FIG 2.3The input plane, warped by the hidden layer
Left: the original input space, where XOR's classes are interleaved. Right: the same grid and points after passing through a trained hidden layer. Notice: at the end of the morph the two blue points have been pushed onto the same spot, while the violet points stay apart. In this new space, a straight line separates them trivially — and that straight line is all the output neuron ever needed to be.
Key idea

Every layer of a deep network is a coordinate change. Depth is repeated re-representation: each layer hands the next one a space in which the problem is a little bit easier, until the final layer's job is a straight cut.

2.4 · Watch it learn

Now the real thing. A 2→4→1 network, initialized randomly, trained by the exact gradient descent you will meet in Lab 3 and the back-propagation of Lab 4. The decision surface starts as a meaningless smear and resolves into the XOR pattern; the loss curve falls beside it.

FIG 2.4Training a network to solve XOR
Left: the network's output over the input plane. Right: training loss, log scale. Notice the plateau: for the first few hundred epochs the loss barely moves while the hidden units organize themselves. Then the boundary snaps into place and the loss falls off a cliff. Compare the single neuron — it plateaus at 0.25 and stays there forever.

2.5 · What each hidden unit actually learned

Open up the trained network. Each hidden neuron is still just a line — the thing we proved was insufficient. But the output neuron gets to combine those lines, and a combination of half-planes can carve out the diagonal region XOR requires.

FIG 2.5Four lines, one solution
Small panels: what each hidden unit responds to — a single straight cut, exactly as Fig 1.2 promised. Large panel: the output neuron's weighted combination of them. Notice: typically two units do most of the work — one firing on the upper-left region, one on the lower-right. Their weighted sum is high exactly where the inputs differ.
Lab 03

Gradient-Based Learning

Learning is rolling downhill on a surface you cannot see, feeling only the slope beneath your feet. This lab makes that surface visible — and shows how one number, the learning rate, decides whether you arrive, crawl, or fly off the edge.

↳ notebook: DL_Lab3_Gradient_Descent_and_Loss.ipynb

3.1 · The slope tells you which way to go

Fix every parameter but one, and plot the loss against it. You get a curve. The derivative at your current position is the slope of the tangent line: positive means the loss rises as you increase the weight, so you should decrease it. That is the whole rule, and it has one minus sign:

the update rule w ← w − η · ∂L/∂w

The step is proportional to the slope, so the descent naturally slows as it approaches the bottom, where the slope flattens toward zero.

FIG 3.1One step of gradient descent
The ball sits at your current weight. The tangent shows the slope; the arrow shows the step you take — always opposite the slope. Notice: near the minimum the tangent flattens, the arrow shortens, and the steps become tiny. Gradient descent decelerates into the valley without being told to.

3.2 · The learning-rate race

The learning rate η scales every step. Too small and you crawl; too large and you overshoot the minimum, land higher up the opposite wall, and overshoot again — worse each time, until the loss explodes to infinity. For the simple bowl L(w) = w² the threshold is exactly η = 1. Below it you converge, above it you diverge.

Run three balls down the same hill with three learning rates.

FIG 3.2Too small, just right, too large
Three learning rates, identical starting point. Dots mark successive steps. Notice: the green ball takes many small steps and is still descending. The violet ball converges quickly. The rose ball's steps grow every iteration — it is climbing the walls, not descending them.
In practice

An exploding loss (or NaN) almost always means the learning rate is too high. Divide it by ten and try again — this is the single most common fix in deep learning.

3.3 · Descending a landscape

Real networks have millions of weights, so the loss surface lives in millions of dimensions. Two dimensions is enough to build the intuition. Here the loss is drawn as a contour map — like a topographic map, where each ring is a constant height.

The gradient is a vector of all the partial derivatives. Two facts about it are worth internalizing: it points in the direction of steepest ascent, and it is always perpendicular to the contour lines. Gradient descent simply walks the other way.

FIG 3.3Contours, the gradient field, and the path down
Rings are equal-loss contours; arrows are the negative gradient field; the path traces the descent. Click anywhere to start from there. Notice: on the stretched bowl the path zig-zags. It crosses the narrow valley repeatedly instead of running along it — because the steepest direction is not the direction of the minimum. This is exactly the pathology that momentum and Adam are designed to cure.

3.4 · Choosing the loss: MSE vs cross-entropy

The loss defines what "wrong" means, and its gradient defines how loudly the network is told about it. For regression we use squared error. For classification we use cross-entropy:

two error functions MSE: L = (ŷ − y)²
Cross-entropy: L = −[ y·log ŷ + (1−y)·log(1−ŷ) ]

Consider a model that is confidently wrong: the true label is 1 and it predicts 0.01. MSE calls that an error of about 0.98 — bounded, unremarkable. Cross-entropy calls it 4.6, and climbing toward infinity. That difference is the point.

FIG 3.4The penalty, and the pressure to fix it
Left: the loss as the prediction sweeps from 0 to 1 (true label = 1). Right: the magnitude of the gradient — the size of the correction the network receives. Notice: when the model is confidently wrong, MSE's gradient vanishes (the sigmoid has saturated), so it barely learns. Cross-entropy's gradient grows. Pairing sigmoid output with cross-entropy cancels the saturating term exactly — which is why nobody trains classifiers with MSE.

3.5 · Bumps, plateaus and bad starts

Textbook bowls have one minimum. Real loss surfaces are lumpy: they have local minima you can get stuck in, saddle points where the gradient nearly vanishes but you are not at a minimum, and long flat plateaus where progress stalls.

Drop several balls from different starting points on the same bumpy curve and watch where they end up.

FIG 3.5Where you start decides where you land
Identical dynamics, different initializations. Notice: gradient descent is entirely local — it never sees the global picture, only the slope underfoot. In the very high dimensions of real networks, true local minima turn out to be rare; saddle points and plateaus are the real obstacle, which is why momentum-based optimizers help so much.
Lab 04

Back-Propagation

Gradient descent needs ∂L/∂w for every weight. Back-propagation is the algorithm that computes all of them in a single backward sweep. It is nothing but the chain rule — applied with enough bookkeeping to be fast.

↳ notebook: DL_Lab4_Backpropagation_from_Scratch.ipynb

4.1 · A network is a computational graph

Forget layers for a moment. A network is a graph of tiny operations — multiply, add, squash — each with an input and an output. Running data through it left to right is the forward pass; each node stores the value it produced, because the backward pass will need it.

Our specimen is deliberately tiny: one input, one hidden unit, one output, four parameters.

the tiny network h = σ(w₁x + b₁) y = σ(w₂h + b₂) L = ½(y − t)²
FIG 4.1The forward pass, node by node
Values flow forward and are cached at every node. Notice: nothing about the forward pass mentions gradients. It just computes — and remembers.

4.2 · The chain rule, in pictures

Suppose you want to know how sensitive the loss is to w₁, a weight buried at the front. Nudging w₁ changes z₁, which changes h, which changes z₂, which changes y, which changes L. The chain rule says: multiply the local sensitivities along the path.

chain rule along the path ∂L/∂w₁ = (∂L/∂y) · (∂y/∂z₂) · (∂z₂/∂h) · (∂h/∂z₁) · (∂z₁/∂w₁)
        = (y − t) · y(1−y) · w₂ · h(1−h) · x

Each factor is trivially computable at its own node using values cached on the way forward. Think of gears: turn the first one and the motion passes down the chain, scaled at each meshing.

FIG 4.2Local gradients multiply backwards
Step backwards and watch the running product accumulate from the loss toward the weight. Notice: every factor here is a number between roughly 0 and 1 for sigmoid — which foreshadows §4.6. Multiply enough of them and the product goes to zero.

4.3 · One full step of learning

Now put it together: forward to get the loss, backward to get every gradient, then update every parameter and re-run forward to confirm the loss went down. Step through it and watch the numbers.

FIG 4.3Forward → backward → update
The complete training loop for one example. Blue values flow forward; rose gradients flow backward; the weights then move against their gradients. Notice: after the update the loss is strictly lower. Repeat this a few thousand times over many examples and you have trained a network. That is all training is.

4.4 · Why back-propagation is fast

You could compute each gradient independently, re-walking the network from the loss to that weight every time. For n parameters that is O(n) full traversals. Back-propagation instead computes each node's gradient once, stores it, and reuses it for everything upstream — one backward sweep yields every gradient.

The saving is not marginal. It is the difference between training a model and not.

FIG 4.4Recompute vs reuse
Work required to obtain all gradients, as the network grows. Notice: the naive approach scales with the number of parameters; back-prop's cost is roughly the same as a single forward pass, regardless of how many parameters there are. This is the algorithmic insight that made deep learning possible.

4.5 · Trust, but verify

Hand-derived gradients are easy to get subtly wrong, and a wrong gradient does not crash — it just trains badly. The fix is the gradient check: perturb a weight by a tiny ε and measure the loss change numerically, then compare to your analytic formula.

numerical gradient (central difference) ∂L/∂w ≈ [ L(w + ε) − L(w − ε) ] ⁄ 2ε
FIG 4.5Analytic vs numerical gradients
Bars compare the gradient from back-propagation against the brute-force numerical estimate for each parameter. Notice: they agree to many decimal places — relative error below 1e-7. Also notice the sweet spot in ε: too large and the approximation is crude; too small and floating-point round-off destroys it.

4.6 · Vanishing and exploding gradients

Return to the chain rule. To reach layer 1 in an L-layer network, the error signal is multiplied by L local derivatives. If each is around 0.2, then after 10 layers the signal has been scaled by 0.2¹⁰ ≈ 1e−7 — the early layers receive essentially nothing and stop learning. This is the vanishing gradient problem, and it is why sigmoid networks could not go deep.

If instead each factor exceeds 1, the product explodes and training blows up. ReLU's derivative of exactly 1 sits on the knife edge — which is precisely why it works.

FIG 4.6The gradient's journey backwards through depth
Gradient magnitude arriving at each layer, on a logarithmic scale, propagating from the output back to the input. Notice: with sigmoid the signal collapses by orders of magnitude per layer — the first layers of a deep net are effectively frozen. With ReLU it holds steady. Every deep-learning trick you have heard of — ReLU, careful initialization, batch norm, residual connections — exists to keep this line flat.
Lab 05

Deep Feedforward Networks

How much can a network represent, how much should it, and what separates memorizing the training data from actually learning the pattern?

↳ notebook: DL_Lab5_Deep_Feedforward_Networks.ipynb

5.1 · Universal approximation, built from kinks

The universal approximation theorem states that a network with a single hidden layer can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy, given enough hidden units. It sounds abstract. It is not — you can watch it happen.

Each ReLU unit contributes one kink: a hinge that is flat until its threshold, then rises with some slope. Add hinges together, with the right weights, and you can trace any curve you like. Add units one at a time and watch a target function assemble itself.

FIG 5.1Building a curve out of ReLU hinges
Grey: the target function. Violet: the network's fit. Faint lines: the individual hinge contributions. Notice: with 2 units the fit is a crude polyline. By 8 it is recognizable. By 30 it is nearly exact. The theorem says this never stops improving — but it says nothing about how many units you need, nor whether training will find them.

5.2 · Depth versus width

If one hidden layer suffices in theory, why go deep? Because "enough units" can mean exponentially many. A deep network composes features: layer one builds simple pieces, layer two combines them into motifs, layer three into objects. Each layer multiplies the number of distinguishable regions rather than adding to it.

FIG 5.2Regions carved by a wide net vs a deep net
Left: one wide hidden layer — each unit adds a fold. Right: the same total number of units arranged in several layers — folds compose with folds. Notice: the deep arrangement partitions the space into far more regions from the same budget of neurons. Depth buys expressiveness exponentially; width buys it linearly.

5.3 · Capacity, underfitting and overfitting

Expressiveness cuts both ways. A model with too little capacity cannot represent the pattern — underfitting. A model with too much can pass through every training point exactly, including the noise — overfitting. It then performs beautifully on data it has seen and badly on data it has not.

Sweep the number of hidden units through the whole range and watch both the fit and the two error curves.

FIG 5.3Too simple, just right, too flexible
Left: the fitted curve against the noisy training points and the true pattern. Right: training error (falls forever) and test error (falls, then rises). Notice: training error is a liar. It keeps improving as you add capacity, long after the model has stopped learning anything real. Only the test curve reveals the sweet spot.
Key idea

The goal was never to fit the training data. The goal is to fit the process that generated the training data. Noise belongs to the sample; signal belongs to the process.

5.4 · The generalization gap and early stopping

The same story unfolds over training time. Early on, both training and validation loss fall together: the model is learning signal. Later, training loss keeps falling while validation loss turns upward: the model has started memorizing. The distance between the two curves is the generalization gap.

Early stopping is the cheapest regularizer ever invented — simply keep the weights from the epoch where validation loss was lowest.

FIG 5.4The moment to stop
Two curves over epochs, with the optimal stopping point marked. Notice: past the marker, more training makes the model strictly worse at its actual job. The training loss gives you no warning whatsoever — it looks like continued progress.

5.5 · Regularization: preferring simpler explanations

Instead of shrinking the model, we can leave it large and discourage it from using all its flexibility. Weight decay (L2 regularization) adds a penalty proportional to the size of the weights:

with weight decay Ltotal = Ldata + λ · Σ wᵢ²

Large weights are what allow a curve to swing wildly between points. Penalize them and the model, all else being equal, prefers the smoother explanation. Turn λ up and watch an overfitting model relax.

FIG 5.5Weight decay tames a flexible model
The same over-parameterized model at increasing regularization strength. Notice: at λ = 0 the curve contorts to hit every noisy point. As λ grows the fit straightens out, test error improves, then eventually the model becomes too rigid and underfits again. There is an optimum — and it is found on validation data, never on the training set.
Where this leaves you

A neuron is a weighted vote plus a bend. A layer is a coordinate change. Depth is repeated re-representation. Learning is descending a slope you feel but cannot see, and back-propagation is the bookkeeping that tells every weight its share of the blame. Everything else — convolutions, attention, transformers — is a smart choice about which weighted sums to take.
