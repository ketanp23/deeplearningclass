01
Make more data · §7.4

Dataset Augmentation

The best way to make a model generalize is to train it on more data. When you can't collect more, you can manufacture it: take each training example and apply transformations that change the input but keep the correct answer the same. A model that has seen a cat rotated, shifted, brightened and flipped ten ways learns that "cat" survives all of those — an invariance you got for free.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: you only have one photo of your dog, but you want your friend to recognise the dog from any angle. So you make copies: one tilted a bit, one moved to the corner, one brighter, one darker, one mirror-flipped. Now there are lots of "different" pictures that are all still your dog.

That's dataset augmentation. The computer takes each training picture and quietly makes many slightly-changed versions, so it sees far more examples than you actually collected. The one rule: the change must not change the answer. Rotating a photo of a dog is fine — it's still a dog. But flipping the letter "b" turns it into "d", so for reading letters you're not allowed to flip. Pick only the changes that keep the label true.

Good transforms, by data type
  • Images = flips, crops, rotations, scaling, colour/brightness jitter, small elastic warps, cutout.
  • Audio = time shift, pitch shift, speed change, background-noise mixing.
  • Text = synonym swaps, back-translation — carefully, meaning must survive.
  • Any model = injecting small input noise is itself a form of augmentation (next section).
The cardinal rule

A transform is legal only if it preserves the label.

  • Horizontal flip = fine for "cat vs dog"; wrong for "b vs d" or "left vs right".
  • Big rotation = fine for aerial photos; wrong for "6 vs 9".
  • Rule of thumb = only apply a change your classifier should be blind to. Augmentation teaches exactly those invariances.
Careful. Never augment the validation or test set with random transforms — you'd be grading the model on made-up data. Augment training only.
In practice Computer vision

Every serious image model is trained with augmentation. The standard ImageNet recipe is a random resized crop, a random horizontal flip and a little colour jitter — that alone buys several points of accuracy. Modern pipelines automate the choices with RandAugment and AutoAugment.

It's the difference-maker on small datasets: a medical-imaging model with only a few hundred scans leans on rotations, flips and elastic deformations to behave as if it had many times more data.

Beyond vision: SpecAugment (time/frequency masks) for speech; back-translation and synonym swaps for NLP; time and pitch shifts for audio.
02
Add noise on purpose · §7.5

Noise Robustness

Deliberately adding noise during training makes a model tougher. Noise can be injected in three places, each with a different effect: on the inputs (a form of augmentation that, for many models, equals a weight penalty), on the weights (pushes the solution toward flat, stable regions), and on the outputs as label smoothing (stops the model from becoming dangerously over-confident).

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: if you only ever practise catching a ball thrown perfectly straight, one wobbly throw will fool you. But if your friend throws it a little wobbly on purpose during practice, you learn to catch it no matter what. A bit of "mess" in practice makes you steady in real games.

Noise robustness is practising with a little mess on purpose. Sprinkle small random changes on the inputs, or on the model's own knobs, or soften the answers from a hard "100% sure" down to "90% sure." Each kind of sprinkle stops the model from clinging too tightly to the exact practice examples, so it stays calm and sensible when the real world is a bit different from practice.

On the inputs

Add small random noise to each input. For a linear model this is provably equivalent to an L² penalty on the weights — the noise variance plays the role of α.

On the weights

Perturb the weights with noise each step. This favours solutions sitting in flat valleys, where small weight changes barely change the output — a hallmark of robust minima.

On the outputs

Real labels can be wrong. Label smoothing replaces hard targets (1, 0, 0) with soft ones (e.g. 0.9, 0.05, 0.05) so the model never chases the impossible certainty of exact 0 and 1.

Input and weight noise appear too: Gaussian input noise is a cheap augmentation, and weight noise nudges training toward flat minima that generalise better.

Typical setting: label_smoothing = 0.1 in the cross-entropy loss — a staple of image-classification and machine-translation training.
03
Borrow from unlabeled data · §7.6

Semi-Supervised Learning

Labels are expensive; raw data is cheap. Semi-supervised learning uses a small pile of labeled examples together with a large pile of unlabeled ones. The unlabeled data reveals the shape of the input distribution — where the clusters are — and a good decision boundary should run through the empty valleys between clusters, not slice through their middle.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine a classroom photo where only two kids are named for you, but you can see everyone. Even without names, you notice the kids naturally stand in two friend-groups. To split "group A" from "group B", you'd draw your line down the empty aisle between the groups — not straight through the middle of one huddle.

Semi-supervised learning does exactly this. A few examples have labels (names); tons of examples don't. The unlabeled ones show where the crowds gather, and the model puts its dividing line in the gap between crowds. So a handful of labels plus lots of unlabeled data can beat the handful of labels alone.

The key assumptions
  • Cluster assumption = points in the same cluster share a label; boundaries live in low-density regions.
  • Manifold assumption = data lies on a lower-dimensional surface; nearby points on it share labels.
  • Shared representation = learn features h that make P(x) and P(y|x) both easy — the unlabeled data shapes h.
Why it regularizes

With few labels, many boundaries fit the labeled points equally well. The unlabeled data breaks the tie, ruling out boundaries that cut through dense regions. That extra constraint is the regularization — you've added information without adding a single label.

When it fails. If the clusters don't actually line up with the classes, the unlabeled data can mislead. The assumptions must hold.
In practice When labels are expensive

Semi-supervised learning shines when unlabeled data is plentiful but labels cost real money or expertise — medical images, speech, web-scale photos. Methods like FixMatch and pseudo-labeling reach near fully-supervised accuracy using a tiny fraction of the labels.

Google's Noisy Student pushed ImageNet state-of-the-art by training on a small labeled set plus 300 million unlabeled images.

Where it shows up: any domain where collecting inputs is easy but labeling needs a doctor, a linguist, or hours of human time.

Learn several jobs at once · §7.7

Multitask Learning

Train one model to do several related tasks at the same time. The lower layers become a shared representation that must work for every task, while each task keeps its own small output head. Because those shared features are pooled from all tasks' data and pressured to be useful for all of them, they generalize better than features learned for any one task alone.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: a student who plays football, basketball and tennis builds strong general fitness — speed, balance, quick reactions — that helps in every sport. Someone who only ever plays one game misses that shared base.

Multitask learning is one brain learning several related jobs together. The bottom part of the network learns general skills useful for all the jobs (like the athlete's fitness), and only the very top splits into a little piece per job. Sharing that base means more total practice flows into the general skills, so the model learns them better — and each individual job benefits.

When it helps
  • Related tasks = they must share underlying structure; forcing unrelated tasks to share can hurt (negative transfer).
  • Data-poor tasks = a task with few examples rides on features learned from data-rich siblings.
  • Auxiliary tasks = even a "fake" extra task can be added purely to shape better shared features.
In practice Shared backbones

Multitask learning is everywhere a single model must do several related jobs. A self-driving perception network shares one backbone and simultaneously predicts detection, lane segmentation and depth — cheaper and more robust than three separate models.

In NLP, T5 casts every task as text-to-text and trains them together; MT-DNN fine-tunes one encoder across many language tasks at once.

Bonus trick: add an auxiliary task purely to shape better shared features — even a 'fake' objective can regularize the backbone.
05
Stop at the right moment · §7.8

Early Stopping

As training proceeds, training error keeps falling but validation error falls, bottoms out, then climbs as the model starts memorizing. Early stopping is the simplest, most-used regularizer of all: watch the validation error, and keep the weights from the moment it was lowest. It costs almost nothing and quietly limits how far the weights drift from their starting point — much like an L² penalty.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: you're revising for a test. For a while, more studying helps. But past a point you start memorising the exact practice questions word-for-word, and you actually do worse on the real test because it asks things slightly differently. The smart move is to stop revising at the moment you were doing best on fresh practice questions.

Early stopping is that smart move for a model. It keeps a set of "fresh" questions aside (the validation set), checks its score on them as it trains, and remembers the exact moment that score was highest. When the score starts slipping, it stops and goes back to that best moment. Simple, cheap, and it works.

How it's done
  • Validation set = hold out data the training never touches; measure error on it periodically.
  • Patience = don't stop at the first uptick; wait p checks without improvement to ride out noise, then roll back to the best.
  • Keep the best = save a checkpoint whenever validation improves; restore it at the end.
Why it's a regularizer

Stopping after t steps limits how far the weights can travel from their small initial values — the number of steps behaves like an inverse regularization strength. For a simple quadratic loss it can be shown to be equivalent to L², with more training ≈ smaller α (a weaker leash).

Bonus. It's practically free — you were computing validation error anyway — and it automatically finds the right amount of training instead of guessing.
In practice In every training loop

Early stopping is probably the most-used regularizer of all, because it's baked into the tools. Keras has an EarlyStopping callback; every Kaggle notebook uses it; gradient boosting exposes it directly as early_stopping_rounds in XGBoost and LightGBM.

The standard setup: monitor validation loss, keep going while it improves, and roll back to the best checkpoint once it hasn't improved for a set patience.

Typical setting: patience = 5–20 evaluations, restore_best_weights = True — free regularization you were already paying for.
06
Force parameters to agree · §7.9

Parameter Tying & Parameter Sharing

Sometimes we know in advance that different parameters ought to be related. Parameter tying adds a penalty that pushes two parameter sets to stay close, ‖wA − wB‖². Parameter sharing goes further and forces them to be exactly equal — the same weights reused in many places. Sharing is the powerhouse behind convolutional networks, and it slashes the parameter count while baking in an invariance.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: to find every cat in a big photo, you don't need a separate pair of "cat-spotting eyes" for the top-left corner, another for the middle, another for the bottom-right. One good "cat detector" slid across the whole picture does the job everywhere. Reusing the same detector is far cheaper than building a fresh one for every spot.

Parameter sharing is reusing the same set of weights in many places instead of learning new ones each time. It saves a huge amount of memory, and it teaches the model a useful truth: a cat is a cat wherever it appears in the image. Parameter tying is the gentler cousin — instead of forcing two weight sets to be identical, it just nudges them to stay similar.

Dense: every output pixel connects to every input pixel (n²·n²·m weights). Shared conv: just k²·m weights, reused at every position. The gap is often thousands of times — that's the regularizing power of sharing.

In practice CNNs, RNNs, Transformers

Parameter sharing is the backbone of modern architectures. CNNs share one kernel across every image position; RNNs reuse the same weights at every timestep; and language models tie the input embedding to the output softmax, saving millions of parameters and often improving quality.

Siamese networks (face verification, similarity search) share weights between their two towers so both inputs are embedded the same way.

Where it shows up: weight tying in GPT-style models; shared conv kernels; twin-tower retrieval and verification systems.

Average many models · §7.11

Bagging & Ensemble Methods

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains several models on different resamples of the data and averages their predictions. Because each model makes somewhat different errors, averaging cancels the random part and keeps the signal. If the models' errors were perfectly independent, the ensemble's variance would drop by a full factor of k — the number of models.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: ask one friend to guess how many sweets are in a jar and they might be way off. Ask twenty friends and average their guesses — the too-high and too-low mistakes cancel out, and the average is usually surprisingly close. A crowd of rough guessers beats one guesser.

Bagging is a crowd of models. You make several models, each trained on a slightly different shuffle of the data, so they make different mistakes. Then you average their answers. The shared, correct part adds up, while the random mistakes cancel — so the team is steadier and more accurate than any single model. The one catch: it only helps if the models actually disagree; twenty identical guessers tell you nothing new.

Var = v · [ 1/k + (k−1)/k · ρ ]

If ρ = 0 (fully independent errors) variance falls to v/k. If ρ = 1 (identical models) it never improves. Real ensembles live in between — diversity is everything.

How bagging builds diversity
  • Bootstrap samples = each model trains on a resample (sampled with replacement), so it sees a different slice of data.
  • Different everything = diversity also comes from different initializations, architectures, or feature subsets (random forests).
  • Average or vote = regression averages outputs; classification votes or averages probabilities.
Bagging vs boosting
  • Bagging = parallel, independent models; reduces variance; models are equals.
  • Boosting = sequential; each model fixes the last one's mistakes; reduces bias, can build a strong learner from weak ones.
Cost. An ensemble of k models is k times the compute and memory at test time. Dropout, next, gets much of the benefit for almost free.
In practice Random forests & Kaggle

Bagging's most famous child is the random forest — hundreds of decision trees, each on a bootstrap sample and a random feature subset, then averaged. It's a default first model for tabular data because it's robust and needs little tuning.

At the top of Kaggle competitions, ensembling many diverse models (blending, stacking) is almost universal — diversity between models is what wins.

Where it shows up: RandomForest / ExtraTrees for tabular data; model blending and stacking in competitions; snapshot ensembles in deep learning.

Bagging on a budget · §7.12

Dropout

Dropout randomly switches off a fraction of units on every training step, so each step trains a different thinned sub-network — but all sub-networks share their weights. It approximates bagging an astronomically large ensemble of networks at almost no extra cost. At test time you keep every unit and scale the weights, cheaply averaging the whole ensemble.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: a football team where, in every practice, a few random players sit out. Because you never know who'll be missing, every player has to learn to do a bit of everything — no one can slack off and rely on a single star. Come match day the whole team plays, and it's far more resilient.

Dropout benches random "neurons" on each training step, so the network can't lean on any one of them. Each step is like training a slightly different mini-network, and there are a mind-boggling number of possible mini-networks — but they all share the same weights, so it's basically free. When it's time to actually use the model, everyone plays at once, turned down a little so the totals stay fair.

Train vs test
  • Training = multiply each unit by a 0/1 Bernoulli mask (keep-probability 1 − p); a different mask every step.
  • Testing = use all units, but scale activations so the expected total matches training. Inverted dropout does the scaling during training instead, leaving test-time untouched.
  • Weight sharing = the 2ⁿ possible sub-networks all reuse one weight set, so training one trains many.
Why it works
  • Cheap bagging = approximates averaging an exponential ensemble at the cost of one network.
  • No co-adaptation = units can't rely on specific partners always being present, so each learns a robust, independently-useful feature.
  • Typical rates = ≈ 0.5 for hidden units, ≈ 0.2 for inputs; too high starves the network.
In practice Dense layers & Transformers

Dropout is standard in dense layers and throughout Transformers — BERT and GPT use dropout 0.1 in their attention and feed-forward blocks. It was the trick that let big fully-connected nets train without overfitting.

A neat second use: Monte-Carlo dropout leaves dropout on at test time and runs the model several times to get an uncertainty estimate — valuable in safety-critical settings.

Typical setting: p ≈ 0.1 in Transformers, ≈ 0.5 in large dense layers, ≈ 0.2 on inputs.

Why it's hard · Chapter 8

The Difficulty of Training Deep Networks

Every technique so far assumes you can actually train the network. Deep models make that assumption fragile. Stacking many layers multiplies gradients together, so they tend to vanish to nothing or explode to infinity; the loss surface is riddled with saddle points, plateaus, cliffs and ill-conditioned regions. Understanding these obstacles is what motivates good initialization, normalization and architecture.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine a message whispered down a line of 50 people. If everyone whispers just a little softer, by the end there's nothing left to hear. If everyone shouts a little louder, by the end it's a deafening, garbled roar. Getting a clear message all the way through a long line is genuinely hard.

Training a deep network passes a "learning signal" back through many layers, and the same thing happens: the signal can fade to nothing (vanishing gradients) so the early layers barely learn, or blow up (exploding gradients) so training goes haywire. The loss landscape is also full of flat plains and tricky dips where progress stalls. That's why researchers invented careful starting values, normalization tricks, and shortcut connections — to keep the message clear from one end to the other.

The obstacles
  • Vanishing / exploding gradients = products of many layer Jacobians shrink or blow up exponentially with depth.
  • Saddle points = in high dimensions these vastly outnumber local minima; gradients near them are tiny, so progress crawls.
  • Plateaus & cliffs = long flat regions stall learning; sudden steep cliffs cause huge, destabilizing jumps.
  • Ill-conditioning = very different curvatures in different directions make a single learning rate a poor fit everywhere.
  • Poor initialization = bad starting scales put the network in the vanishing or exploding regime from step one.
The fixes
  • Careful init = Xavier / He scaling keeps the per-layer gain near 1 so signals neither die nor blow up.
  • Normalization = batch / layer norm re-centres and re-scales activations, smoothing the loss surface.
  • Residual connections = skip paths let gradients flow directly, defeating vanishing in very deep nets.
  • Gradient clipping = caps exploding gradients so a cliff can't wreck training.
  • Good optimizers = momentum and adaptive methods (Adam) cope with saddles and ill-conditioning.
The link back. Set g = 1.0 in the bench: the signal holds steady all the way down. That's exactly what good initialization and normalization are engineered to achieve.
In practice What made deep learning work

This section is the story of modern deep learning's breakthroughs. ReLU + He initialization kept signals alive through many layers; Batch Normalization (2015) smoothed the loss surface enough to make very deep nets trainable; residual connections let ResNets pass 100 layers by giving gradients a shortcut.

For recurrent and Transformer models, gradient clipping tames exploding gradients and Adam copes with saddle points and ill-conditioning. Training GPT- or ResNet-scale models means using essentially all of these at once.

The toolkit: He/Xavier init · BatchNorm or LayerNorm · residual connections · gradient clipping · Adam/AdamW — the reason depth is trainable at all.

Key Takeaways

If you remember only one thing

Regularization is any move that trades a little training performance for better generalization. Beyond shrinking weights you can add data (augmentation), add doubt (noise, dropout, early stopping), add structure (sharing, multitask, semi-supervised), or add models (bagging). And none of it matters unless you can get the gradients to flow — which is why training deep nets is its own hard problem.

01

Augmentation manufactures data

Apply label-preserving transforms to expand the training set and teach invariances for free. Never augment validation or test.

02

Noise toughens models

Input noise ≈ weight decay; weight noise favours flat minima; label smoothing curbs over-confidence.

03

Unlabeled data is free information

Semi-supervised learning puts the boundary in low-density gaps between clusters, using cheap unlabeled data to break ties.

04

Sharing tasks shares features

Multitask learning pools data into a shared trunk; the representation must serve all tasks, so it generalizes better.

05

Early stopping is free L²

Keep the weights from the validation minimum. Limiting training steps limits weight travel — like a weaker leash the longer you train.

06

Sharing beats tying beats nothing

Tying nudges weights close (‖w_A−w_B‖²); sharing forces them equal. Convolutions share one kernel everywhere, slashing parameters.

07

Bagging averages away variance

Diverse models on bootstrap resamples; variance falls toward v/k only if errors are uncorrelated. Diversity is the whole point.

08

Dropout is cheap bagging

Random masks train 2ⁿ weight-sharing sub-networks; scale at test time. Prevents co-adaptation for the cost of one network.

09

Depth fights back

Gradients vanish or explode, saddles and ill-conditioning stall progress. Good init, normalization and residuals keep signals alive.
