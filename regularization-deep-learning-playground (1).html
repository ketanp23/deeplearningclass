ST 01
Departure · The objective

What Regularization Is

Regularization is any modification we make to a learning algorithm that is intended to reduce its generalization error but not its training error. The most classical form does this by adding a penalty on the size of the parameters directly to the cost the optimizer minimizes — trading a little fit for a lot of faith that the model will hold up on unseen data.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine teaching a puppy to walk to you across a park. If you just let it run, it might sprint past you and dash into the road. So you clip on a leash — the puppy still moves toward you, but a gentle backward tug stops it going too far.

Regularization is that leash for a learning machine. The "puppy" is the model's weights — the numbers it uses to make decisions. Left alone, they run hard to match every tiny detail of the practice questions, which is like memorizing answers instead of really learning (grown-ups call that overfitting). The leash is a small rule that tugs those numbers back toward zero, so the model learns the main idea and still does well on new questions it has never seen. The knob called α (alpha) is how tightly you pull: α = 0 means no leash at all, and a bigger α means a firmer tug.

A parameter norm penalty augments the objective with a term Ω(θ) that grows as the weights grow, scaled by a hyperparameter α that sets how hard the penalty pulls.
Regularized objective
J̃(θ; X, y) = J(θ; X, y) + α · Ω(θ) ,    α ∈ [0, ∞)
The two forces
  • Data term J = the ordinary loss (cross-entropy, MSE…). Its gradient pulls the weights toward w*, the fit that minimizes training error.
  • Penalty term αΩ = a spring back toward the origin. Its gradient pulls the weights toward 0.
  • α is the dial = α = 0 recovers the unregularized problem; larger α weights the penalty more, shrinking the weights. It is a hyperparameter you tune on a validation set, not learned.
  • The solution w̃ = sits where the two gradients cancel — not at w*, not at 0, but at the balance point.
Why penalize weights, not biases An exam favourite

The vector w is the weights; θ = {w, b} is everything. We normally penalize only w and leave the biases b alone.

  • Weights = each couples two variables, so fitting one well needs seeing both under many conditions — prone to overfit, worth regularizing.
  • Biases = each controls a single variable, so it is fit accurately from far less data. Regularizing it mostly just adds underfitting for no benefit.
  • Per-layer α = in principle a separate α per layer; searching them all is expensive, so a single α is a reasonable default.
Frame it right. Regularization is not "make the model worse." It deliberately raises training error a little so that the gap between training and test error shrinks more. The target is always generalization.
In practice The core problem

The moment you reach for regularization: your model scores 99% on the training set but 82% on validation. That gap is overfitting, and every method in this course exists to close it.

It bites hardest when data is scarce. Train a CNN on a few thousand chest X-rays and it memorises them in a handful of epochs — perfect on what it has seen, useless on the next patient. Regularization is what turns that memoriser into a model a radiologist can trust on new scans.

Rule of thumb: a big train–validation gap ⇒ add regularization or data; poor scores even on training ⇒ the model is too small (underfitting) instead.
ST 02
The workhorse penalty

L² Regularization — Weight Decay

The most common penalty is half the squared L² norm of the weights. In statistics it is ridge regression or Tikhonov regularization; in deep learning it is weight decay. Its whole effect on a single gradient step is beautifully simple: shrink the weights a touch, then do the usual update.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: think of each of the model's numbers as air in a balloon that has a tiny slow leak. Every single step of learning, a little air escapes, so the number gets a bit smaller before the model does its normal work.

That quiet shrinking is the whole trick, and it is why we call it "decay" — the same way a battery slowly drains or a balloon slowly deflates. Each step, every weight is multiplied by something just under 1 (say 0.99). Do that thousands of times and the big, showy numbers fade away — unless the data keeps a good reason to pump them back up. Nothing gets yanked to zero all at once; it just gets gently smaller, again and again.

L² penalty and objective
Ω(θ) = ½‖w‖22 = ½ wᵀw   ⟹   J̃(w) = J(w) + (α/2) wᵀw
Where "decay" comes from

The gradient of the regularized objective just adds αw:

∇w J̃ = ∇w J + αw

So a single gradient-descent step (learning rate ε) becomes:

w ← w − ε(∇wJ + αw)
   = (1 − εα) w − ε ∇wJ

Before every ordinary update, the weight vector is first multiplied by a constant factor just under 1. It decays. That single multiplication is the entire mechanism.

Weight decay in code
loss = data_loss(y_hat, y) + (alpha/2) * (weights ** 2).sum()   # explicit penalty

# or, equivalently, fold it into the optimizer:
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=eps, weight_decay=alpha)
# each step does:  w <- (1 - eps*alpha) * w  -  eps * grad
Watch the coupling. Because the decay is (1 − εα), the effective regularization depends on the learning rate. Change ε and you silently change how hard weight decay bites — one reason optimizers like AdamW separate the two.
In practice The default dial

Weight decay is the most-used regularizer in deep learning — a one-line argument in every optimizer. A ResNet on ImageNet trains with weight decay near 1e-4; a small model on a small dataset might use 1e-2.

One catch practitioners hit: with Adam, naïve L² and true weight decay differ, which is exactly why AdamW ('decoupled weight decay') exists and is now standard for training Transformers.

Typical setting: weight_decay = 0.01 with AdamW; sweep 1e-4 → 1e-2 and pick the value with the best validation score.

L² Through the Hessian

To see what L² actually keeps and what it throws away, approximate the loss near the optimum w* by a quadratic bowl. The curvature of that bowl — the Hessian H — decides everything. L² rescales each eigen-direction of H independently, and the rescaling has a clean closed form.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: the model is trying to find the lowest spot in a valley by rolling a ball. Some valleys are steep and narrow like a deep bowl; others are wide and flat like a dinner plate. In a steep bowl the ball really wants the exact bottom — push it and it rolls straight back. On a flat plate the ball doesn't care where it sits — nudge it and it just stays put.

Regularization is a gentle push toward the middle (toward zero). On the steep directions that push barely moves the ball, because the valley fights back — those numbers clearly matter, so we keep them. On the flat directions the push slides the ball a long way toward zero, because nothing was holding it there — those numbers weren't doing much, so we let them shrink. The fancy word Hessian is just a way of measuring how steep the valley is in each direction.

Quadratic approximation of J around its optimum w*
Ĵ(w) = J(w*) + ½ (w − w*)ᵀ H (w − w*)   ⟹   regularized optimum   w̃ = (H + αI)−1 H w*
In the eigenbasis H = QΛQᵀ, direction i is rescaled by a factor
w̃i = λi / (λi + α) · w*i

Which directions survive

The factor λ/(λ+α) is near 1 when λ ≫ α and near 0 when λ ≪ α. So L²:

  • Keeps steep directions = large λ (high curvature). Moving off w* here spikes the loss, so these weights genuinely reduce the objective — they are barely touched.
  • Decays flat directions = small λ (low curvature). The loss hardly cares about them, so they contribute little and get shrunk toward 0 with almost no cost.
  • Half-life at α = λ = when α equals an eigenvalue, that direction is cut exactly in half.

Application — L² on linear regression

For linear regression the normal equations pick up an αI on the diagonal:

ordinary: w = (XᵀX)−1 Xᵀy   →   ridge: w = (XᵀX + αI)−1 Xᵀy

Since XᵀX is proportional to the input covariance, adding αI inflates the apparent variance of every feature. Weights on features that already show high variance (and covary with the target) are hardly moved; weights on low-variance features are shrunk hardest — the same "keep steep, decay flat" story in the language of data.

In practice Ridge regression

This is ridge regression's day job. When input features are correlated — economic indicators, neighbouring pixels, genomic markers that move together — plain least-squares becomes wildly unstable: tiny data changes swing the coefficients hugely.

Adding the L² term αI fixes the ill-conditioning. The flat, redundant directions get pulled toward zero while the well-determined directions are barely touched — exactly the eigenvalue story in the bench above.

Where it shows up: multicollinearity in finance, econometrics and biology; the α on the diagonal is what makes the matrix safely invertible.

L¹ Regularization & Sparsity

Swap the squared norm for the plain sum of absolute values and the behaviour changes qualitatively. L¹ does not merely shrink weights — it drives many of them to exactly zero, performing feature selection as a side effect. This is the LASSO.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: your pencil box is crammed with 50 pencils, but you really only use about 8. There are two ways to tidy up. L² sharpens every pencil down a little, so you still have all 50, just slightly shorter. L¹ is stricter — it says "if you barely use a pencil, throw it out completely," so you end up with just your 8 favourites and 42 empty slots.

Those empty slots are weights set to exactly zero, and having lots of zeros is called sparsity. It's useful because the model then ignores the inputs it doesn't need and tells you plainly which ones actually mattered. Why can L¹ throw things all the way to zero while L² only trims? Because L¹ keeps pushing with the same strength no matter how small a number gets, so it finishes the job — while L²'s push gets weaker as the number shrinks, so it never quite reaches zero.

L¹ penalty and gradient
Ω(θ) = ‖w‖1 = Σi |wi|   ⟹   ∇wJ̃ = ∇wJ + α · sign(w)

Notice the penalty gradient is α·sign(w) — a constant push toward zero whose size does not shrink as w shrinks. Under a diagonal-Hessian approximation the solution has a famous closed form, soft thresholding:

Soft-threshold solution (H diagonal, Hi,i > 0)
w̃i = sign(w*i) · max{ |w*i| − α / Hi,i ,   0 }

Why sparsity, in one line. L²'s push toward 0 is αw — it fades as the weight shrinks, so it never quite finishes the job. L¹'s push is α·sign(w) — a constant that keeps shoving until the weight is pinned at 0.
What sparsity buys you
  • Feature selection = zeroed weights mean their inputs are ignored entirely. The model tells you which features it doesn't need.
  • Interpretable, compact models = fewer active parameters to store, reason about and deploy.
  • LASSO = Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator — L¹-penalized linear regression, the canonical sparse estimator.
L¹ in code
l1 = alpha * weights.abs().sum()       # Σ|w_i|
loss = data_loss(y_hat, y) + l1

# proximal / soft-threshold step (ISTA):
w = w - eps * grad_data                # gradient of data term
t = eps * alpha
w = sign(w) * clamp(abs(w) - t, min=0) # shrink toward 0, clip at 0

The clamp(abs(w) − t, min=0) is the dead zone: anything smaller than the threshold t is set to exactly zero.

In practice Feature selection

Reach for L¹ (LASSO) when you have far more features than you need and want the model to tell you which matter. Given thousands of candidate genes, or hundreds of financial signals, LASSO drives the useless coefficients to exactly zero and keeps a short, readable list.

The same trick powers model compression: L¹ pressure during training prunes weights away, shrinking a network for phones and embedded devices without a big accuracy hit.

Where it shows up: genomics & finance feature selection, sparse coding, network pruning — anywhere 'which inputs actually matter?' is the question.

Comparison · Bayesian view

L¹ vs L² & the Priors Behind Them

Both penalties fall out of the same Bayesian idea: put a prior on the weights that says "small is more probable," then do MAP estimation. The shape of that prior is exactly what separates shrinkage from sparsity.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: before you even look at the data, you can already have a hunch about what the answer should look like — like guessing a new friend's height before you meet them. L² and L¹ are two different hunches.

The L² hunch is a smooth round hill: "the numbers are probably smallish, none of them crazy-big." The L¹ hunch is a sharp mountain peak sitting right on zero: "most numbers are probably exactly zero, and only a few really matter." That sharp peak at zero is exactly why L¹ likes to set weights to zero — the hunch itself is already betting on zero. Grown-ups call this starting hunch a prior (what you believed prior to seeing the data), and mixing your hunch together with the data is called MAP.

MAP estimation maximizes log p(y | X, w) + log p(w). The log-prior log p(w) is the negative penalty −αΩ(w). A Gaussian prior gives L²; a Laplace prior gives L¹.

The corner is the point. Go back to the contour bench and switch to L¹: the diamond's corners sit on the axes, so a loss contour usually first touches the region at a corner — and a corner means one coordinate is exactly zero. The sphere has no corners, so L² almost never lands a weight on an axis.
In practice Elastic net

In practice you rarely have to choose: Elastic Net mixes L¹ and L² so you get sparsity and stability. It's the go-to when features come in correlated groups — pure LASSO would keep one member of a group at random and drop the rest, while the L² part keeps the group together.

The Bayesian 'prior' view is a practical guide: expect a few strong effects ⇒ lean L¹ (Laplace prior); expect many small diffuse effects ⇒ lean L² (Gaussian prior).

Typical setting: ElasticNet with l1_ratio ≈ 0.5; sweep it on validation to slide between ridge-like and lasso-like behaviour.

The other reading · §7.2

Norm Penalties as Constrained Optimization

There is a second way to see every norm penalty: as a hard constraint that the weights must stay inside a region, Ω(w) < k. The two views are linked by a Lagrangian, and each choice of α corresponds to some region size k — you just don't get to see k directly.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: there are two ways to stop kids wandering too far in a park. Way one: every time a kid drifts off, you call out and nudge them back, and the nudge gets stronger the further they go — that's a penalty. Way two: you draw a chalk circle and say "play anywhere you like, but stay inside this circle" — that's a constraint.

Here's the neat part: these two are secretly the same thing. A firmer nudge (bigger α) does exactly what a smaller circle does — both keep the kids closer to the middle. So whenever you add a penalty to shrink the model's numbers, you're really just drawing an invisible circle around them, and turning up α quietly shrinks that circle. The circle's size is the k in the formulas; you can't read it off directly, but you always know bigger α means a smaller circle.

Generalized Lagrangian for "minimize J subject to Ω(θ) < k"
𝓛(θ, α) = J(θ) + α (Ω(θ) − k)   ⟹   θ* = argminθ   maxα, α ≥ 0   𝓛(θ, α)
Reading the Lagrangian
  • Fix the optimal α* = and the problem becomes exactly minimizing J(θ) + α*Ω(θ) — the penalized objective from station 01. Penalty and constraint are two faces of one problem.
  • α and k move oppositely = a larger α pushes for a smaller region k; a smaller α allows a bigger region. Crank α up and the weights are confined nearer the origin.
  • k is implicit = we rarely know the exact k a given α implies (it depends on the shape of J), but we know the direction: more α, tighter leash.

Same solution, different knob. You can regularize by adding αΩ to the loss (penalty view) or by optimizing freely and clipping the weights back inside a ball of radius k (constraint view). For the right pairing of α and k, they reach the same w̃.
In practice Tuning α

The constraint picture is how you should reason about the α hyperparameter. Turning α up shrinks the ball the weights may live in, so you tune it like a budget: too tight and the model underfits, too loose and it overfits.

In real projects you don't guess α — you cross-validate it, sweeping a log-scale grid (1e-4, 1e-3, … 1e1) and keeping the value with the best validation score.

Workflow: log-spaced α grid → k-fold cross-validation → pick the validation-optimal α → refit on all data.
ST 07
Reprojection & max-norm

Explicit Constraints & Reprojection

If you take the constraint literally you get a different algorithm: after each gradient step, project the weights back onto the region. This projected gradient descent often behaves better than the equivalent penalty — and it is the basis of max-norm regularization.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: imagine a video game where the map has a boundary wall. You can walk around freely, but the moment you step past the edge, the game snaps you straight back to the boundary. That snap-back is called reprojection: take a normal step, and if the numbers grew too big, pull them right back to the edge of the allowed circle.

Why do this instead of just nudging (the penalty way)? Two reasons. First, snapping back never accidentally squashes everything to zero and gets the model stuck. Second, it stops the numbers from blowing up out of control. A popular version called max-norm does the snap-back for each part of the network separately, so no single piece is ever allowed to shout much louder than the others.

Why explicit constraints can beat penalties
  • No dead zones = a penalty can create flat regions near the origin where non-convex optimization stalls with all-tiny weights. A reprojection never encourages weights toward the origin, so it avoids those dead spots.
  • Stability = penalties can trigger a runaway feedback loop — large weights make large gradients that make larger weights — so weights blow up. Reprojection caps the norm and prevents divergence regardless of learning rate.
  • Max-norm per column = Hinton and colleagues (in the dropout work) recommend constraining the norm of each column of a weight matrix separately, so no single hidden unit acquires enormous weights. You reproject column by column.
Max-norm in code
# after optimizer.step(), reproject each column to norm <= c
with torch.no_grad():
    norms = weights.norm(dim=0, keepdim=True)        # per-column (per-unit) norm
    scale = (c / norms).clamp(max=1.0)               # only shrink, never grow
    weights *= scale                                 # reproject onto the ball
In practice Training stability

Explicit norm constraints earn their keep when training is unstable. Max-norm was famously paired with dropout so you could push the learning rate high without weights blowing up. Its close cousin, gradient clipping, is standard when training RNNs and Transformers to survive gradient spikes.

Weight-norm constraints also stabilise GANs and reinforcement-learning agents, where a single runaway weight can derail an entire training run.

Where it shows up: max-norm ≈ 3–4 with aggressive dropout; gradient clipping (e.g. clip norm to 1.0) in nearly every large-model training loop.

Regularization & Under-Constrained Problems

Some models simply have no unique answer without regularization. When there are flat directions in the data — no variance, or fewer examples than parameters — the matrix you need to invert is singular and the optimum runs off to infinity. A norm penalty rescues these under-constrained problems by making the answer well-posed.

In plain words GRADE 6

The picture: some puzzles just don't have one clear answer. If I only tell you "two numbers add up to 10," you can't know which two — is it 3 and 7? 5 and 5? There are endless answers, so the puzzle is under-constrained: not enough clues to pin it down.

Machines hit the same wall, and there's a sneaky version too. If a model can perfectly separate two groups of dots with a line, it starts thinking "if I make my numbers even bigger, I look even more sure" — so the numbers grow forever and never settle, like someone shouting louder and louder for no real reason. Regularization fixes both by adding a tiny nudge toward small numbers. Suddenly there's one sensible answer, and the shouting stops at a normal volume. Adding that little bit — the α on the diagonal — is exactly what turns an impossible puzzle into a solvable one.

The singular matrix, fixed by αI

Linear regression, PCA and many linear models invert XᵀX. That matrix is singular when the data has no variance in some direction, or when m < n (fewer examples than features).

(XᵀX)−1   may not exist  →  (XᵀX + αI)−1   always exists (α > 0)

Adding α to every diagonal entry lifts every eigenvalue by α, so the smallest can no longer be zero. The problem becomes invertible and stable.

The pseudoinverse as a regularization limit

The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse — the tool that gives a stable answer to under-determined linear systems — is exactly the vanishing-regularization limit of ridge regression:

X+ = limα → 0+ (XᵀX + αI)−1 Xᵀ

Read it as: regularize, then let the leash go slack. What remains is the minimum-norm solution — the well-behaved answer regularization was quietly selecting all along.

In practice The p ≫ n regime

The under-constrained case is everyday reality whenever there are more features than samples (p ≫ n): text with huge vocabularies, genomics with 20,000 genes and 200 patients, tiny fine-tuning sets. Without regularization there is no unique solution at all.

The separable-data trap is just as real: logistic regression on perfectly separable classes sends its weights to infinity. A dab of L² gives a finite, sensible answer — which is why libraries switch it on by default.

Good to know: scikit-learn's LogisticRegression is L²-regularized out of the box (C is the inverse strength) precisely to stop weights diverging.

Key Takeaways

If you remember only one thing

A norm penalty is a second force on the weights. The data term pulls toward the fit w*; the penalty pulls toward 0; the solution w̃ is the balance. Everything else — weight decay, ridge, LASSO, constraint balls, reprojection, the pseudoinverse — is just which shape of pull you choose and how hard you pull.

01

Penalty, not punishment

Regularization reduces generalization error, not training error. J̃ = J + αΩ. α is a validation-tuned dial, and we penalize weights, not biases.

02

L² is a per-step shrink

Weight decay multiplies w by (1 − εα) before each gradient step. The effective strength is entangled with the learning rate.

03

Curvature decides survival

w̃ = (H + αI)⁻¹Hw*. Each direction is scaled by λ/(λ+α): steep directions (large λ) are kept, flat ones (small λ) are decayed away.

04

L¹ is soft thresholding

w̃ᵢ = sign(w*ᵢ)·max(|w*ᵢ| − α/Hᵢᵢ, 0). A constant push α·sign(w) pins small weights at exactly 0 — sparsity, aka LASSO.

05

Priors behind the penalties

L² = MAP with a Gaussian prior; L¹ = MAP with a Laplace prior. The Laplace cusp at 0 is where sparsity comes from.

06

Penalty ⇄ constraint

Via the Lagrangian J + α(Ω−k), a penalty of strength α equals a constraint of some region size k. More α means a smaller region, weights nearer the origin.

07

Explicit constraints are steadier

Reproject after each step instead of penalizing. Avoids dead zones near the origin, prevents runaway divergence, and enables per-column max-norm.

08

Regularization makes problems well-posed

XᵀX + αI is always invertible; separable logistic regression gets a finite solution; the pseudoinverse is the α → 0 limit of ridge.
